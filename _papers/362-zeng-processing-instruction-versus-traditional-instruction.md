title: >
  Processing Instruction Versus Traditional Instruction:
  The Transfer-of-Training Effects on Chinese EFL Learners
authors: >
  Tao Zeng, Chang Xu, Jia Hu, and Xiuzhi Fu
paper_link: "https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440241231037"
abstract: >-
  This research investigated the impact of processing
  instruction (PI) on the acquisition of the English
  third-person singular present tense by Chinese
  English-as-a-foreign-language (EFL) learners and whether
  this effect would extend to the acquisition of other
  language components. This study featured the
  pretest-immediate-posttest design and participants were
  required to participate in all tests (pretest, immediate
  posttest, delayed posttest). Two classes were assigned to
  receive PI and Traditional Instruction (TI) respectively,
  while another class served as the control group. A
  language background check was conducted 2 weeks before
  the main experiment. And after three consecutive class
  hours, there is an immediate posttest and a delayed
  posttest in 2 weeks. By comparing the results of the
  three tests, the study measured and compared the primary
  effects, secondary transfer-of-training (TOT) effects,
  and cumulative TOT effects of PI and TI. The findings
  showed that both PI and TI had significant effects,
  although they differed in the extent and duration of
  improvement in acquisition effectiveness. PI was found to
  be superior to TI, which could be reflected in primary
  and two TOT effects except the interpretation tasks
  related to passive voice. This study suggests that PI can
  be utilized in grammar teaching due to its efficacy in
  teaching specific linguistic structures and its
  transfer-of-training effects on other grammar rules.

      Detailed Analysis:

      Between the two active treatment arms (PI and TI), the
      paper explicitly states that treatment duration,
      difficulty, and number of activities were kept as
      consistent as possible (quote 4 above; this sentence
      follows the description of PI/TI post-testing and is
      read as referring to the two instructed arms, since the
      control group has no "teaching materials" of its own),
      so there is no imbalance between PI and TI. The more
      delicate question is the control group, which received
      no explicit grammar instruction. However, the study's
      design explicitly frames "explicit instruction" itself
      as the variable under investigation: the control
      condition is deliberately the "business-as-usual"/
      no-instruction baseline used to show that PI and TI
      produce effects beyond mere exposure (Tables 4-6 compare
      both instructed groups against this control). The
      authors also state they matched the total amount of
      linguistic-item exposure (reading/listening texts
      containing the target forms) across all three groups,
      withholding only the explicit teaching component that
      is the explicit treatment variable being tested. Under
      the ERCT decision rule, when the extra resource (here,
      explicit instruction) is itself the treatment variable
      being investigated, the control may legitimately lack
      that resource as long as this is the clearly stated
      intent, which is the case here.

      Criterion B is met because the withheld resource
      (explicit grammar instruction) is the explicit treatment
      variable under study, the control's business-as-usual
      status is clearly intentional, and exposure to the
      linguistic items was otherwise kept equivalent across
      groups.
