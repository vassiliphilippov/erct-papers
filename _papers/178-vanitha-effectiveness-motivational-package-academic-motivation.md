abstract: >-
  Background: In education, academic motivation both
  intrinsic and extrinsic plays a critical role in student
  engagement, learning strategies, and achievement.
  However, challenges such as low engagement, academic
  stress, and fluctuating motivation continue to hinder
  outcomes. To address this, the present study evaluated
  the effectiveness of a Motivational package in improving
  academic motivation and performance among nursing
  students. Methods: A quasi-experimental study was
  conducted with 80 nursing students, equally divided into
  control and intervention groups. Baseline
  socio-demographic and academic variables were assessed.
  The intervention group received a two-week MEP comprising
  lectures, presentations, time and stress management
  strategies, positive behaviour therapy, videos, group
  activities, and humour/laughter therapy. Academic
  motivation was measured using the AMS-C 28 scale,
  alongside amotivation and academic performance
  indicators. Pre-tests and post tests were administered
  to both groups. Results: Post-intervention, the
  intervention group showed a significant increase in
  motivation scores (118.30 vs. 131.53; p < 0.001) and a
  reduction in amotivation. Good academic performance rose
  from 27.5% to 60%. The control group showed no
  significant changes. Conclusion: The motivational package
  effectively improved academic motivation and performance
  among nursing students.

      Detailed Analysis:

      Criterion B requires comparing the nature, quantity,
      and quality of resources (time, budget, materials)
      given to intervention versus control groups, unless
      additional resources are themselves the explicit
      treatment variable. The intervention group received
      substantial additional educational time and resources:
      a structured motivational package delivered two hours
      per day, three days a week, over two weeks (roughly 12
      hours of additional facilitated sessions), plus
      lectures, videos, group activities, and therapy
      components. The control group received nothing
      additional ("business as usual"); the paper describes
      no equivalent time, activity, or resource provided to
      the control group.

      Applying the decision tree: extra resources are present
      (added facilitated hours and materials) and the
      difference is not negligible (about 12 hours of extra
      structured sessions). The key question is whether these
      additional resources are the explicit treatment
      variable being tested. The paper explicitly names "the
      motivational package" as the independent variable and
      states the study's aim is to "evaluate the
      effectiveness of a Motivational package" - i.e., the
      motivational package as a whole, including its added
      time and activities, IS the intervention being tested
      against a business-as-usual baseline. The added time
      and resources are integral to and inseparable from the
      package being evaluated, not a separable confounding
      add-on layered on top of a different core intervention.
      Under the standard's logic (a study explicitly testing
      the impact of a resource package as the primary
      treatment variable may leave the control at
      business-as-usual), this allows Criterion B to be met,
      while clearly noting that the intervention group
      received considerable additional facilitated time and
      materials that the control group did not.

      Criterion B is met because the additional time and
      resources (the structured motivational package) are the
      explicit treatment variable being tested against a
      business-as-usual control, rather than a separable,
      confounding add-on.
