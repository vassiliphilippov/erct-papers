abstract: >-
  In this article, we explore oral and reading
  development in Spanish and English for a sample of
  70 first grade Hispanic English-learning boys and
  girls receiving a longitudinal English intervention
  and a comparison group of 70 boys and girls. Students
  were assessed at the outset of kindergarten and first
  grade, and the exit of first grade. Results showed
  that, on average, treatment students scored
  significantly better in dual oracy and Spanish
  literacy than control students. Girls demonstrated a
  faster rate in dual reading comprehension than did
  their boy counterparts. When the effects of treatment
  and gender were jointly examined, it is apparent that
  the treatment effect contributed to a larger
  proportion of variance compared to gender.

      Detailed Analysis:

      This criterion was flagged for extra scrutiny
      because it was a point of disagreement among the
      initial blind rankings. The paper consistently
      refers to whole "treatment classrooms" and
      "control bilingual classrooms" as the pools from
      which the 70-plus-70 analytic sample was drawn,
      confirming that condition status was held at the
      classroom level: no quote anywhere in the paper
      suggests a treatment student and a control
      student ever shared one classroom, which is the
      specific contamination risk criterion C targets.

      However, on close re-reading, the word "random"
      in quote (2) modifies the SAMPLING of 140
      analytic students out of the larger project
      population ("for a balanced design and to allow
      for missing data"), not the ASSIGNMENT of
      classrooms to treatment or control. The paper
      never quotes an explicit randomisation procedure
      (e.g., a lottery or random draw applied to
      classrooms or schools) explaining how a classroom
      came to be labeled "treatment" versus "control."
      The only support for randomised assignment (as
      opposed to some other allocation method) is the
      paper's own generic label of the larger project
      as "randomized," which this specific article does
      not unpack further; the randomisation mechanics
      may be documented in a separate, paywalled
      companion methods paper (e.g., Tong, Irby,
      Lara-Alecio, & Mathes, 2008) that could not be
      verified from this article's text alone, and per
      the verification procedure this criterion is
      judged from this paper's own text rather than by
      searching companion papers.

      Weighing both points: the paper gives strong,
      consistent evidence that condition was assigned
      at whole-classroom granularity (satisfying the
      anti-contamination purpose of the criterion), and
      it explicitly self-describes the parent project as
      "randomized," but stops short of quoting the exact
      randomisation procedure. On balance, the totality
      of indirect evidence supports "met," though this
      remains a closer call than most criteria in this
      paper and the underlying ambiguity legitimately
      explains why raters split on it.

      Criterion C is met because classrooms, not
      individual students within one classroom, held
      treatment/control status, and the paper
      self-identifies as part of a randomized project,
      even though no single quote spells out the
      randomisation mechanics.

      and training is integral to the treatment variable
      being tested, rather than a separable confound.
      The paper's central research questions concern the

      delivered by trained teachers ("focused and direct

      are the operational definition of the structured

      contrasts against a "typical practice"

      state/district-mandated minimum. This matches the
      RESOURCES_ARE_TREATMENT branch of the current ERCT
      decision tree for criterion B: the study explicitly
      tests a structured, resourced instructional package
      against a business-as-usual control, so the control
      group's lower resourcing is by design rather than an
      unaddressed confound.
